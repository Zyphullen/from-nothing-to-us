Qudits & pairs

we thinkThe links of the chain · size No size: comes before space

A qudit is a tiny quantum unit with d states.

Two qudits share one perfect entangled state.

That pair is one "link".

Alex says

A qudit has dimension 2 or more (2 = qubit, 3 = qutrit).

A pair is "maximally entangled": as linked as quantum rules allow.

A shared qudit is the end of 2+ pairs. It carries one copy per pair (because entanglement is "monogamous").

Recipe

2 qudits → 1 link. Links join at shared qudits → a network.

A less-than-perfect pair would need smooth, continuous numbers. A fully discrete theory cannot have them.

Virtual Cell (a typical human cell)

Typical human cell (generalised)

we think

A general human body cell, about 20 µm across: it has the parts most human cells share.

No single real cell type looks exactly like this; the shape is simplified.

Real human cells range from about 7 µm (red blood cells) to over 100 µm, and from round to long and branched.

Sizes and speeds of the parts inside are real measured values, drawn to scale.

Some counts are reduced so you can see between things: proteins at ~1/8 of real crowding, 30 of ~10,000 vaults, fewer microtubules and ribosomes.

Sources: Cell (Wikipedia) · Cell Biology by the Numbers

Cell membrane

known

A skin of fat molecules two layers thick, about 5 nm.

It lets some things in and keeps others out.

Made of roughly a billion lipid molecules, with proteins floating in it.

In cells that crawl, the front edge ruffles and moves forward a few µm per minute (a lamellipodium).

Zoom right in to see the two layers of lipids.

Sources: Cell membrane (Wikipedia) · Lamellipodium (Wikipedia)

Nucleus

known

The control centre: about 10 × 8 µm here (typically 5–10 µm).

It holds 46 chromosomes: about 2 metres of DNA.

About 6.2 billion DNA letters (base pairs) in two copies of the genome.

Wrapped in a double membrane with a few thousand pores.

Under a microscope (🔬 button) it glows blue, like the DAPI dye biologists use to stain DNA.

Sources: Cell nucleus (Wikipedia) · Human genome (Wikipedia)

Chromatin (DNA)

known

DNA wound on protein spools (nucleosomes).

Each spool holds 147 DNA letters, wrapped almost twice.

A nucleosome is about 11 nm wide and made of 8 histone proteins.

About 30 million nucleosomes pack the genome into the nucleus.

In living cells the beads fold into irregular chains 5–24 nm wide, not a neat uniform 30 nm fibre.

Sources: Nucleosome (Wikipedia)

Nucleosome

known

A spool of 8 histone proteins with DNA wrapped around it.

About 11 nm wide.

147 base pairs wrap about 1.65 turns.

Linker DNA joins one spool to the next, like beads on a string.

Drawn simplified: DNA wrapped around histone beads. The two twisted strands are the 2 nm double helix; the wrapping around each bead is not drawn.

Sources: Nucleosome (Wikipedia)

Nucleolus

known

A dense spot inside the nucleus, 1–3 µm.

It builds the parts of ribosomes.

Most human cells have 1–4. It has no membrane: it forms where ribosome genes cluster.

Ribosome pieces leave through the nuclear pores.

Sources: Nucleolus (Wikipedia)

Nuclear pore

known

A gate in the nucleus wall, about 120 nm across.

mRNA goes out, proteins come in.

One of the biggest machines in the cell: ~1,000 proteins of ~30 kinds, in 8-fold symmetry.

A human nucleus has roughly 2,000–5,000 pores.

Sources: Nuclear pore (Wikipedia)

Ribosome

known

A protein-building machine, about 25–30 nm.

It reads mRNA and adds about 5–6 amino acids per second.

A human cell has millions (estimates around 10 million in fast-growing cells).

Two parts: a small (40S) and a large (60S) subunit.

At 1× speed you can watch the new chain grow at the real rate.

Sources: Ribosome (Wikipedia) · BioNumbers: translation rate

Endoplasmic reticulum

known

A lace of tubes and sheets spreading through the cell.

Makes proteins and fats, stores calcium.

Rough ER (with ribosomes) sits near the nucleus; smooth tubes reach out to the edge.

Tubes are about 50–100 nm wide and keep moving and re-joining.

Sources: Endoplasmic reticulum (Wikipedia)

Golgi apparatus

known

Stacks of flat sacs next to the nucleus: the post office.

It sorts, tags and packs proteins.

In human cells the stacks join into a ribbon of several µm near the centrosome.

Each stack has about 4–8 sacs (cisternae).

Cells that secrete a lot, like fibroblasts making collagen, have big Golgi.

Sources: Golgi apparatus (Wikipedia)

Vesicle

known

A small bubble (50–100 nm) carrying cargo.

Motor proteins walk it along microtubules at about 1 µm per second.

Kinesin walks outwards (~0.8 µm/s), dynein inwards (~1 µm/s).

Off the track, a vesicle only jiggles slowly: the cytoplasm is very crowded.

Sources: Vesicle (Wikipedia) · Kinesin (Wikipedia)

Mitochondrion

known

The power station: makes ATP.

A tube about 0.5 µm wide and 1–10 µm long; a human cell has hundreds.

They form a network that splits and joins every few minutes.

The folds inside (cristae) hold ATP synthase, a spinning motor.

It has its own small DNA: it came from a bacterium long ago.

Sources: Mitochondrion (Wikipedia)

ATP synthase

known

A rotary motor about 10 nm wide in the cristae.

It spins over 100 times a second to make ATP.

Protons flowing through it turn the rotor.

Each turn makes 3 ATP.

Sources: ATP synthase (Wikipedia)

ATP

known

The cell’s energy coin.

Made in mitochondria, spent everywhere.

A human body makes and recycles roughly its own weight in ATP every day.

Sources: Adenosine triphosphate (Wikipedia)

Lysosome

known

A recycling bag full of enzymes, 0.1–1 µm.

A cell has a few hundred.

Its inside is acidic (pH about 4.5–5); a pump uses ATP to push protons in.

Its enzymes work best in acid, so any that leak into the near-neutral cytoplasm (pH ~7.2) do little harm.

Sources: Lysosome (Wikipedia)

Peroxisome

known

A small bag (0.1–0.5 µm) that burns fats.

It makes and destroys hydrogen peroxide.

Human cells have a few hundred.

Also helps make some special fats for the brain.

Sources: Peroxisome (Wikipedia)

Lipid droplet

known

A store of fat, 0.5–2 µm.

Wrapped in a single layer of lipids.

Cells use them as an energy store and to make membranes.

Sources: Lipid droplet (Wikipedia)

Centrosome

known

Two tiny barrels (centrioles), 250 nm wide and 500 nm long.

Microtubules grow out from here.

Each barrel is 9 groups of 3 tubes.

In most human cells the older barrel also grows a primary cilium, a tiny antenna.

It helps pull chromosomes apart when the cell divides.

Sources: Centrosome (Wikipedia)

Microtubule

known

Hollow tubes 25 nm wide: roads and scaffolding.

Each tip grows ~12 µm/min, then suddenly shrinks.

This "dynamic instability" is simulated here with measured rates.

The bright tip is where new tubulin is added (biologists see it with EB1 tags).

Kinesin and dynein walk on them carrying cargo.

Sources: Microtubule (Wikipedia) · Dynamic instability (Wikipedia)

Actin stress fibres

known

Bundles of actin filaments (each 7 nm) running across the cell.

They pull on the ground like ropes.

They end at focal adhesions, the cell’s "feet".

A single filament is two strands twisted, crossing every 37 nm.

Sources: Stress fiber (Wikipedia) · Microfilament (Wikipedia)

Focal adhesion

known

A cell’s “foot” that grips the surface, 1–5 µm long.

Stress fibres pull on it.

Made of integrins and many linking proteins.

Feet at the front form and mature in minutes.

Sources: Focal adhesion (Wikipedia)

Vimentin filaments

known

Rope-like filaments, 10 nm wide.

They cushion the nucleus and help the cell resist stretching.

Vimentin is the main intermediate filament of many cell types (e.g. fibroblasts); others use keratins.

Sources: Vimentin (Wikipedia)

Vault

we think

A hollow barrel of protein and RNA, about 40 × 70 nm.

What it does is still not clear.

About 10,000 per cell (some cells have more).

Ideas include transport or defence, but no job is proven.

Sources: Vault (organelle) (Wikipedia)

Cytosol

known

The gel that fills the cell: about 70% water.

Packed with roughly 3 million proteins per cubic µm.

So crowded that big things barely drift unless motors carry them.

Small molecules still diffuse fast: across the cell in seconds.

Sources: Cytosol (Wikipedia) · Cell Biology by the Numbers

Water molecule

known

One oxygen atom and two hydrogen atoms (H₂O).

About 99 of every 100 molecules in a cell are water.

Real water molecules jiggle and tumble trillions of times a second; here they are shown frozen or hugely slowed.

Water’s slightly charged ends help proteins fold.

Sources: Properties of water (Wikipedia)

Glucose

known

A sugar: a ring of carbon and oxygen.

Cells break it down for energy.

Glycolysis in the cytosol starts the job; mitochondria finish it and make ATP.

Sources: Glucose (Wikipedia)

Phospholipid

known

A water-loving head and two oily tails, about 2.5 nm long.

Two layers of them make every membrane.

The tails hide from water, so lipids form two layers by themselves.

A kink in one tail keeps the membrane fluid.

Sources: Phospholipid (Wikipedia)

Base pair (DNA)

known

Two DNA letters joined in the middle.

Each side hangs on a sugar-phosphate backbone.

A pairs with T, G pairs with C, held by weak hydrogen bonds.

Pairs are stacked 0.34 nm apart along the helix.

Sources: Base pair (Wikipedia)

Protein chain

known

Amino acids linked in a chain by a ribosome.

The chain then folds into a working shape.

There are 20 kinds of amino acid; the gene sets their order.

The backbone repeats N–C–C; side chains stick out.

Sources: Peptide (Wikipedia)

Protein

known

A folded chain of thousands of atoms, typically 3–10 nm.

Proteins do most of the work in a cell.

Enzymes, motors, pumps and scaffolds are all proteins.

Shown as simple blobs: real shapes are far more detailed.

Sources: Protein (Wikipedia)

Hydrogen atom

Made just after the Big Bang.

About 6 in 10 atoms in your body are hydrogen.

Made inside stars.

15 protons.

known

Protons and neutrons packed tight.

  • brain → Nerve cell (neuron): Body about 4–100 µm, with long branches; the brain has about 86 billion. source

Origin key

  • Big Bang: Made in the first 20 minutes.
  • Cosmic-ray splitting: Fast particles break bigger nuclei.
  • Dying low-mass stars: Giant stars (1–8 Suns) catch neutrons slowly (s-process), then puff off their outer layers. (Most of our lithium: such stars and novae.)
  • Exploding massive stars: Fusion in stars >8 Suns, spread by core-collapse supernovae.
  • Exploding white dwarfs: Type Ia supernovae: lots of iron-group metals.
  • Merging neutron stars: Rapid neutron catching (r-process). Maybe also rare supernovae (debated).
  • Radioactive decay only: No long-lived form; made as other elements decay.
  • Human-made: Made in reactors or accelerators (some also in traces or in stars, briefly).
